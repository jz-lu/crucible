Challenge ladders: correctness, resource costs and honest comparisons
Review date: 2026-10-15

Purpose and limits
------------------
This review asks whether each ladder changes a meaningful problem dimension
while preserving its correctness requirement, and whether its graphs represent
the registered implementation faithfully. It is not independent cryptographic
certification, a proof that higher tiers are harder, or an optimization of every
scheme over all possible parameter choices. A coherent research ladder can
still have avoidable overhead and an incomplete attack model.

Design requirements
-------------------
1. Publish one correctness requirement for each scheme/version ladder. Every
   tier must satisfy that same bound. A smaller actual failure probability or
   larger unused decoding margin at an easier tier is permitted; relaxing the
   required bound to obtain harder challenges is not. Keep generation/resource
   aborts separate, with their conditioning explicit. See correctness-policy-v0.txt.
2. Scale an actual mathematical problem or construction resource. Increasing
   dimensions, field sizes, code lengths or error-correction resources should
   have a defensible connection to the attack problem and to at least one real
   cost. Do not obtain a convincing-looking curve merely by adding padding,
   lowering message entropy at fixed geometry, or spending correctness margin.
3. Prefer reasonably efficient choices within the declared construction and
   correctness envelope. Explain deliberately conservative choices and exact
   published-reference endpoints. Do not claim a globally optimal efficiency
   frontier merely because encoded sizes increase with tier. Nor must every
   individual metric increase: a meaningful tradeoff can affect computation
   or memory while leaving a particular wire length unchanged.
4. Report native parameter vectors, distributions, exact encodings and the
   source of every estimate. A tier is an ordinal position in a versioned
   ladder, not a unit of security. Do not force fifty distinct steps where the
   construction supports only a smaller defensible set of geometries.
5. An attack reference is neither a lower bound nor measured time. State which
   attacks, costs, preprocessing and entropy ceilings it covers. Missing or
   unsupported estimates stay missing. A brute-force ceiling is an upper
   bound on an attack cost, not evidence that the scheme attains that strength.
6. Graphs show this implementation's canonical binary public-key/ciphertext
   lengths, excluding JSON, hexadecimal wrappers and separately registered
   shared domain parameters. They are not optimized minima for a scheme family.
   Key generation, encapsulation and decapsulation graphs remain unpopulated
   until a shared measurement method and actual measurements exist. Bit sizes
   and operation-count arguments do not by themselves justify seconds.
7. Keep issued parameter mappings, receipts and evidence immutable. A changed
   construction or sampling distribution needs a distinct release/profile
   identity. Do not silently reinterpret old recoveries on a replacement ladder.

Retained finite-field DH release 6
---------------------------------
Local sources: crucible/dh_v6.py; scripts/generate_dh_v6_parameters.py;
crucible/calibration_v2.py; tests/test_dh_v6.py.

The field modulus grows from 32 to 3072 bits. The order-q subgroup grows with
it, and full uniform nonzero exponents are sampled independently per challenge.
Public keys and ciphertexts each grow from 4 to 384 bytes. Agreement is perfect
for every honest draw under the exact group arithmetic and transcript encoding.
These are real resource increases, with no correctness relaxation.

The public setup generates p=2*R*q+1, with q proved prime and q^2>p. Pocklington
certificates prove every p and q; they do not certify a discrete-log cost.
The subgroup order doubles as the large certificate factor, which is convenient
for a compact exact proof but is not an optimized subgroup-size selection:

  tier    field bits    subgroup bits    R bits    initial reference bits
     1            32               17        14                  8.299441
    25           678              340       338                 66.818913
    50          3072             1537      1535                128.000000

At tier 50, the generic log2(q)/2 term is about 768.05, while the normalized
prime-field NFS reference is 128. Full-width subgroup exponents therefore spend
substantially more exponent bits than the model needs once its field attack
dominates. This does not make the ladder direction wrong, but it prevents a
claim that its future arithmetic timings represent efficient DH at those
reference strengths. A separately reviewed smaller-subgroup construction or
explicitly specified short-exponent variant could reduce that overhead. Do not
silently restrict the current uniform distribution to obtain such a speedup.

The published relation p=2*R*q+1 is not, by itself, evidence of a special-NFS
weakness. At the high endpoint R is 1535 bits, not a small fixed coefficient.
At the tiny endpoint the entire field is deliberately small. The subgroup is
prime-order, so factoring its cofactor does not split the subgroup order into
small Pohlig-Hellman components. This review did not perform an exhaustive
special-structure attack analysis and does not establish equivalence to
uniformly sampled primes. The exact structured distribution remains disclosed.
Fixed groups also permit reusable preprocessing; reports must include that cost.

The rho/NFS reference uses a project-normalized asymptotic expression anchored
at 3072 bits. It is not a validated finite-size cost estimator, a confidentiality
reduction or an online-only cost. Retain the release and these limitations;
revisit exponent/subgroup efficiency before claiming representative DH timings.

Primary context: RFC 7919 section 5.2 discusses shorter exponents as an explicit
efficiency choice and gives a 275-bit example for its different ffdhe3072 group.
That recommendation is not automatically transferable to Crucible's custom
groups. Appendix A.2 estimates that published group at 125 bits, illustrating
that Crucible's normalized 128-bit anchor is a convention, not a universal fact.
https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7919.html#section-5.2
https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7919.html#appendix-A.2
Pocklington certificates and constructive prime generation are described in
Handbook of Applied Cryptography, chapter 4, sections 4.3 and 4.4.4. Crucible's
fixed recursive factor-size/base-2 sampler is not Maurer's full distribution,
and must not inherit the latter's almost-uniformity claim.
https://cacr.uwaterloo.ca/hac/about/chap4.pdf

Retained RSA release 3
----------------------
Local sources: crucible/rsa_v3.py; crucible/calibration_v2.py;
tests/test_rsa_v3.py.

The modulus grows from 32 to 3072 bits with independently generated balanced
factors. Larger integer arithmetic and ciphertext lengths are real costs.
For distinct proved prime factors and e*d=1 modulo (p-1)(q-1), RSA inversion
works for every honest sampled z, including nonunits. The same perfect-agreement
requirement applies throughout, conditioned on successful bounded generation.
The shared candidate/pair/time limits can abort generation; an abort is not a
completed agreement trial and must not be hidden as success.

Factors have a disclosed recursive Pocklington distribution, not uniformity over
all primes. Private certificates never enter the public transcript. This review
found no basis to assert a specific attack from that structure alone; it also
did not prove this distribution as strong as generic balanced RSA moduli.
The nominal rho/NFS model omits ECM, quadratic sieve and other attacks/constants.
Low-tier values are particularly rough. A selected field size reaching a model
target does not establish that all practical attacks require that work.

The current pk is N||e, padding both to the modulus width even though e=65537
always needs only three bytes. Thus the tier-50 graph reports a 768-byte key,
where this same integer pair could use 384+3 bytes with a different agreed
encoding. Ciphertext size is 384 bytes. This is an avoidable implementation
overhead, not an intrinsic doubling of RSA public-key size. Retain the immutable
encoding now; a future encoding revision should be explicit and independently
identified, including its KDF transcript. Do not replace actual graph values
with hypothetical compact sizes.

The custom transcript KDF and variable-time Python implementation are not
RFC 9690 RSA-KEM compliance or a confidentiality theorem. That RFC supplies
construction context, not admission evidence for this adapter:
https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc9690
Constructive-prime context and distribution cautions:
https://cacr.uwaterloo.ca/hac/about/chap4.pdf

Retained elliptic-curve research release 1
-----------------------------------------
Local sources: crucible/ec_research.py; crucible/ec_parameters.json;
docs/implementation-ec-research.txt; scripts/check_ec_parameters.py;
tests/test_ec_research.py.

Each tier uses a distinct certified prime-order short-Weierstrass curve.
Field sizes grow from 17 to 257 bits; the displayed generic attack reference
is log2(q)/2 for the actual group order, about 8.136488 to 128.239715 bits.
Larger fields and full-width scalars create real encoding and arithmetic costs.
The same exact group identity r(xG)=x(rG) gives perfect honest agreement at
every tier. The published ECPP/Lucas and group-order certificates establish the
algebra used by that argument, not confidentiality or optimized curve choice.

The generator excludes singular curves, j=0/1728, anomalous order and embedding
degrees at most 100. These are useful checks, not an exhaustive curve-security
review. Generic rho references omit curve-specific attacks, constants, reusable
preparation, parallel tradeoffs and quantum algorithms. These custom curves and
the transcript KDF are not X25519 or RFC 9180 DHKEM.

The registered encoding is 0x04||x||y, with full affine coordinates. Public keys
and ciphertexts grow from 7 to 67 bytes. At the endpoint a compressed encoding
could use 34 bytes, but it changes the wire format and decoding obligations.
SEC 1 section 2.3.3 describes compressed and uncompressed point encodings; this
does not certify these custom curves. Retain the current release and disclose
that its graph is the full-point implementation, not an optimized minimum.
https://www.secg.org/sec1-v2.pdf
Published, distinct X25519/DHKEM constructions:
https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7748.html
https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9180.html

Retained Frodo matrix-dimension research release 1
-------------------------------------------------
Local sources: crucible/lattice_frodo.py; docs/lattice-frodo.txt;
docs/lattice-frodo-calibration.json; docs/lattice-frodo-correctness.json;
scripts/lattice_calibrate_frodo.py; tests/test_lattice_frodo.py.

The frozen ladder increases n through 50 distinct multiples of eight from 8
to 640, retaining q=32768, nbar=8, the exact finite noise law and full message,
seed, salt and output distributions of its declared Frodo640 construction.
Canonical public-key size is 16+15*n bytes; ciphertext size is 152+15*n bytes.
The full matrix contains n^2 entries and the main products perform increasing
arithmetic. These are meaningful resource costs. Actual memory and time still
depend on implementation and have not been published as benchmark results.

Every tier meets the common honest-disagreement requirement delta<=2^-133 under
the explicitly declared ideal-XOF sampling model. The proof uses an exact
rational Chernoff bound on each coordinate, then a union bound over 64 decoded
coordinates. Evaluating at n640 covers all smaller dimensions. No independence
between decoded coordinates is needed for that union bound. n8 additionally has
a deterministic support bound giving delta=0; this stronger result does not
change the common requirement. The finite SHAKE seed is not literally an
unbounded independent random tape, so retain the statistical model qualification.

The first two dimensions have no numerical estimate because the selected
lattice estimator is unsupported or hits its small-block-size floor. Later
references use only primal-uSVP and dual-hybrid estimates with a moment-matched
Gaussian approximation to the finite noise law, capped at 128 by message search.
This is not an exhaustive KEM attack model or a security lower bound.

The final published-construction anchor is a deliberate efficiency exception:

  n       uncapped minimum lattice reference    displayed capped reference
  576                              124.684                       124.684
  584                              127.020                       127.020
  592                              129.064                       128.000
  640                              141.912                       128.000

Only n576 and n640 in this table are active late-ladder profiles; the other
rows come from the recorded candidate scan. The model cap is already reached
at n592. Compared with that candidate, n640 uses 720 more public-key bytes and
720 more ciphertext bytes, and about 16.9% more matrix entries. It is therefore
not the minimum-resource parameter attaining the displayed 128-bit cap under
this limited model. Nor does the cap prove n592 has the same actual security
as n640. Keeping the independently byte-checked published n640 endpoint is a
defensible reference choice with broader provenance, not a measured security
improvement inferred from the flat capped number. Preserve this distinction.

Published Frodo parameters and implementations:
https://frodokem.org/
Pinned upstream construction used for the n640 conformance fixture:
https://github.com/microsoft/PQCrypto-LWEKE/tree/a2f9dec8917ccc3464b3378d46b140fa7353320d
Pinned, incomplete attack-estimation baseline:
https://github.com/malb/lattice-estimator/tree/53da5982597709ba0fdf94ea37a84d822310fd84

Recommendations from the four-family review
-------------------------------------------
Retain these four mathematical mappings and their historical evidence. Their
resource direction and common correctness envelopes make sense. This is not
a clean bill of optimal efficiency or comprehensive security estimation.
Before making competitive cross-scheme timing claims, review DH subgroup/
exponent choices, RSA public-exponent padding and EC point compression, then
measure identified implementations under a shared benchmark. Keep the Frodo
anchor choice visible in the detailed specification. Improve attack estimates
as separately versioned evidence rather than rewriting the original records.

Other-family dispositions
-------------------------
Module-lattice: replace the fixed-geometry modulus path with eleven degree/rank
settings. Lossless encodings and minimal prime moduli satisfy the same perfect
correctness requirement. Bytes and arithmetic grow. Native message capacity
grows with degree; the reference includes an explicit message-search caveat.
Six larger settings have independently reproduced flattened-LWE estimates;
smaller estimator-floor cases remain unknown. See lattice-module-v2.txt.

HQC: replace fixed ring/decoder geometry with 27 weights and 23 minimally sized
supported codecs, retaining a worst-case zero-failure bound. Some adjacent
weights share a code because that is a real discretization. The endpoint is a
partial proof/decoder boundary, not a calibrated security target. See
hqc-growth.txt for the proof, source/build limitations and sanitizer coverage.

NTRU Prime: retain the certified degree/weight growth and common perfect bound.
Admissibility can cause small encoding dips. McEliece: retain length growth,
fixed m12/t64 and syndrome size; public matrices and matrix work grow. Neither
family's elementary search-space data is a complete attack estimate. See
code-ntru-ladder-review.txt for sources, tradeoffs and endpoint qualifications.

Current disposition: all eight families have explicit progression, cost,
correctness and endpoint explanations. This is a design audit, not a proof of
monotone security, Pareto optimality or a full security review. The new proposal
gate requires these same explanations and an independent manual assessment;
future submissions do not inherit approval from resemblance to a built-in.
