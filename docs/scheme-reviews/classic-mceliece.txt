Classic McEliece — selection and admission review
Assessment date: 2026-10-14
Decision: KEEP as a research and cryptanalysis reference, with a visible dated
security assessment. Do not present this inclusion as a deployment recommendation.
Live challenge admission: NOT YET APPROVED.

This review examines primary-source specifications and recent attack claims.
It does not independently verify the attack proofs, reproduce recovered keys,
audit an implementation, or run its KATs.

1. Why keep it despite changed security assessments?

Classic McEliece offers a substantially different experiment from HQC: decoding a
public binary Goppa code while the efficient decoding structure is secret. Its
large public keys, small ciphertexts, and exact honest decoding also expose
tradeoffs that HQC and lattice KEMs do not cover in the same way. The official
specification defines a Niederreiter-style KEM with implicit rejection. [M1]

The recent structural cryptanalysis makes this particularly relevant to
Crucible's mission. It is a case where an established security narrative, concrete
cost estimates, small-instance experiments, and implementation reproducibility
can be compared without assuming that a large nominal number is settled fact.
Removing the construction solely because its security assessment is changing
would remove an unusually informative research target.

NIST's March 2025 nonselection considered anticipated use, large public keys,
the concurrent ISO process, and the burden of another standard. It did not mean
that a practical break had been found. [M2] Conversely, those dated conclusions
must not be used as a current blanket security endorsement.

The team reports ISO standardization in June 2026, including mceliece4*, 6*, and
8* profiles and their listed variants, not mceliece348864. [M3] BSI's subsequent
note dated 2026-09-29 recommends against Classic McEliece for new developments,
while expressly stating that the discussed results do not give a practical
attack against its recommended parameter sets. It also emphasizes incomplete
peer review and unresolved realistic cost analysis. [M4]

These statements can coexist: standardized; under intensified cryptanalysis;
not demonstrated practically broken at those full sizes; unsuitable for a
generic "safe deployment" badge on Crucible. A research catalogue is not a list
of schemes we advise people to deploy.

security ladder from their names: length, Goppa degree, field, and attack model
all matter. The mceliece6960119 versus mceliece6688128 contrast is a useful
reminder that a larger length need not mean a larger ciphertext.

3. Assumptions and the meaning of a break

The immediate mathematical target is one-way decoding for the specified
distribution of binary Goppa codes and fixed-weight errors. It is not the
worst-case NP-hardness of arbitrary syndrome decoding. Nor does identifying a
public key as a Goppa code automatically recover a plaintext or shared key.

The team's security analysis explicitly distinguishes one-wayness from a
stronger pseudorandom-code interpretation. Its August 2024 response explains
that a public-code distinguisher alone does not break its stated security
claim. [M6] That logical distinction still matters; that older response is not
a rebuttal of the later 2026 key-recovery papers.

Crucible should preserve three evidence types: public-code distinguishing;
equivalent-secret-key recovery; and recovery of the encapsulated shared key.
Only the last, verified for the issued instance and deadline, directly wins
the current numerical challenge. A recovered equivalent decoding key can
produce that answer; it need not reproduce the generator's exact secret bytes.
No automatic transfer from a family-level result to every registered profile.

4. The 2026 results change the review, with precise qualifications

GIJS, ePrint 2026/1630, received 7 August and revised 27 August: the authors give
a provable quasipolynomial public-code distinguisher in the regime
m=Theta(log n), t=Theta(n/log n), k=Theta(n). They extend it to heuristic
decryption and equivalent-key recovery. Their abstract describes improved
but impractical concrete estimates. The proved distinguisher and heuristic
recovery extensions are different claims. [M7]

Weis, ePrint 2026/1984, version 0.5 revised 6 October: reports a sparse-linear-
algebra run costing 2^89–2^98 bit operations, or 2^107–2^117 under the author's
preferred memory-charging model. An alternative full-memory charging model
gives 2^117–2^128. Two recovery approaches require respectively 100–1400 runs
or one run. The full-size estimates depend on four heuristic assumptions;
full-size runs are not feasible to test. The demonstrated recovered TII-253
instance has m=8, t=9, n=214. [M8]

Saarinen, ePrint 2026/1986, revised 24 September: reports recovery of TII-254,
m=8, t=12, n=223, with an artifact, independent key verifier, and Lean proofs
of reusable linear-algebra steps. Two final Krylov sequences consumed 27.2
GH200 GPU-hours, excluding other processing. These are neither a one-hour
attack nor end-to-end formally verified security claims. [M9]

Assessment: material research advances with concrete small-instance evidence;
not a demonstrated recovery of a named full-size Classic McEliece profile.
The old TII brute-force labels "253" and "254" are not comparable security-bit
certificates. They cannot be copied onto Crucible's tiers or used to say that
256-bit KEM security was practically broken. Message recovery and structural
key recovery also have different baseline attack costs.

The BSI note predates Weis's 6 October revision. Its quoted numerical estimates
therefore must not be silently mixed with the current paper's cost table. [M4,
M8] Store author, revision, success assumptions, memory, preprocessing, target
count, and cost unit alongside every estimate. The different memory models
alone make a single security-bit line misleading.

I checked the official team statement index and its linked earlier responses.
I did not locate there a response adjudicating the October Weis revision.
This is not a claim that no response exists elsewhere. Before admission or a
new public security estimate, a reviewer should reconcile any subsequent
author/team responses and the precise attack version being reproduced.

5. Correctness and implementation evidence

The specified Goppa decoding contract gives perfect honest correctness under
valid generation and exact implementation. [M1] This says nothing about the
hardness of recovering the secret, side-channel resistance, or a buggy parser.
Generation retries caused by the systematic-form requirements must be modeled
as part of key generation, separately from decryption failures and timeouts.

Official implementation choices include portable ref/vec and SIMD variants.
The official integration is libmceliece; the latest listed release on review is
20260622. The project documents compiled-binary timing-flow tests as well as
compiler-induced hazards. [M5, M10] Prefer a pinned official release to an
arbitrary wrapper whose mathematical or encoding version is unclear.

The 2022 round-4 KAT archive and executable Sage specification provide explicit
provenance for conformance work. Sage is intended for readable input/output
testing and is not constant-time. [M11] Admission needs byte-for-byte KAT
reproduction for the selected non-pc profile, agreement across implementations,
padding and malformed-input tests, and the chosen library's own full tests on
the deployment compiler/CPU. These have not been performed here.

The key-size outlier is an infrastructure test, too. Review upload/download
limits, storage, serialization, and hourly generation latency for >1 MB public
keys. Do not solve a deadline problem by silently reusing a secret key across
rounds. Public-domain upstream code does not eliminate our responsibility for
resource isolation and exact adapter provenance.

6. Reduced instances and a useful initial experiment

The mathematical specification permits a principled small-instance study, but
a toy generator is not one of the named deployment profiles. Pin a separate
research release with valid (m,n,t), irreducible Goppa polynomials, distinct
support, full-rank systematic matrices, fixed-weight errors, and unchanged or
explicitly replaced KEM encoding/transform. Test actual agreement rather than
assuming arbitrary shrinking preserves the contract.

First proposed experiment: reproduce a small publicly documented structural
key recovery with its independent verifier, then use the recovered structure to
recover a freshly encapsulated key under our exact reduced KEM contract. This
tests mathematical provenance, method reuse, and the limits of claimed compute
accounting in one coherent exercise. It is a better admission precursor than
drawing fifty points from an untested security estimator.

After that, compare generic message-decoding and structural key-recovery costs
at a small number of reviewed points. Do not guarantee that the same attack
dominates everywhere, that increasing a displayed tier makes every attack
harder, or that the toy-to-full-size extrapolation has been experimentally
established. Historical snapshots should keep the initial reference model and
newer attack estimates separately.

7. Remaining decision and credit gates

Keep one Classic McEliece catalogue slot because its research contribution is
distinct from HQC. Make the recent cryptanalysis visible before offering a
challenge; keep the front card concise and the evidence timeline expandable.
Defer activation until the selected implementation, exact profiles, KATs,
resource costs, and attack-model labels have manual sign-off. Do not present
the 1978 origin of the underlying construction as forty-eight years of
unchanged evidence for today's parameter claims.

Both construction and assumptions are previously identified. Reproducing an
attack or importing a library is valuable attributed work, but neither becomes
a newly discovered scheme nor automatically receives new-family admission
points. Any genuinely changed construction should state its difference from
this baseline and enter the normal private review pipeline.
