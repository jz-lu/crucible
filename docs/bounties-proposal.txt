Bounties: a place to find useful research work
Proposal for review — updated 27 October 2026

The product

Make Bounties a discovery surface for concrete research tasks whose briefs have
been checked. A visitor should quickly recognize an interesting question, see
whether they have the relevant background, and find a credible starting point.
The main experience is exploring open work. Posting, reviewing and reward
administration support that experience without occupying the main screen.

A bounty is a task, not necessarily an entire open research problem. For example,
"reproduce this recorded experiment at these parameters" is a meaningful task
within "does this attack improve on the baseline?" Completing the former need
not settle the latter. A negative result can fulfill the task when its stated
criteria explicitly allow it.

The proposed visual

- Research bounties is the page title. Open describes the displayed tasks' status.
- Open tasks fill a straightforward card board. Each card leads with the question,
  a small Bounty badge using the Notes title-label style, a concrete contribution
  and relevant background. Its footer holds only the numbered spectrum and point
  reward. Placeholder subject labels and the repeated Explore link are removed;
  the whole card opens the task, whose context and source notes remain available.
- The spectrum runs from 1 = open-ended to 5 = concrete. All five numbers are
  visible, with the task's position highlighted. The middle values stay unnamed.
  It is a card annotation; browsing does not require selecting a position first.
- Sort by More concrete first, More open-ended first, Highest points first or
  Lowest points first. The entire collection is sorted before pagination. The
  default is concrete first; ties retain a stable order and unset values go last.
  Every sort retains all open tasks. Ratings and points are illustrative here.
- The spectrum filter, named categories, A/B/C switch and experimental column
  layout have been removed. Search, Find and the directions dropdown remain removed.
  Difficulty, background, reward and the strength of verification are independent
  of the task's position on the spectrum.
- Notes and Bounties share surfaces, colors and tag styling. Notes keeps a denser
  expandable reading list; Bounties keeps the grid. The selected Notes layout is
  A / Simple list: quieter text-font titles and plain row dividers. The temporary
  comparison switcher and the two unused layouts have been removed.
- Selecting a card opens its brief, starting notes and contributions. Returning
  preserves the selected sort and page. An arbitrary task is never opened
  automatically and allowed to consume half the discovery screen.
- Your bounties is a quieter secondary destination: proposed tasks, responses
  awaiting your judgment, and items awaiting the pipeline. Past bounties retain
  the outcomes without competing with open work. A flag marks the visualization.

Keep explanations out of the main browsing path. The card should answer "could
I engage with this?" The selected task should answer "what exactly would help?"
Only the relevant action should explain posting, review or reward rules.

Relationship to Notes — agreed architecture, production implementation pending

A bounty is a distinct task linked to research notes. Calling it a type of note
was too broad: the ordinary note editor, reader and publication workflow do not
supply bounty behavior. Keep a dedicated human-facing task view and posting form
with expected output, completion criteria, verification plan, reward and status.

The bounty record owns versioned task terms, offered points, brief checks,
submissions, poster and pipeline decisions, closure and the reward receipt.
Notes supply the source research and submitted evidence, linked by exact revision.
Reuse notebook authorship, citations, access and revision infrastructure where
appropriate; the choice of storage reuse must not dictate an inadequate task UI.
A note's Bounty badge can link to a related task. An authored tag cannot create
a bounty, confer eligibility or award points.

Notes have permanent numeric references such as #0035 for human discussion and
search. A bounty can display those references beside its source notes. Exact
evidence still pins the note ID, revision and content hash; the short number
alone identifies a note across its revision history. Bounties have their own
identity and do not borrow a cited note's number as their task identifier.

For example, a note reports an attack experiment. A separate bounty asks for an
independent reproduction with specified inputs, outputs and an offered reward.
A contributor submits a result note; the bounty tracks the verification result
and both approvals, then records the award and outcome. Ordinary publication of
the result note does not complete the bounty.

Freeze the task terms when it opens. Editing source or evidence notes does not
silently alter the criteria, reward or approved evidence. Changed terms require
a new checked version with existing claims handled explicitly. Closing the task
preserves its brief and linked research. Several focused bounties may reference
one source note; one contribution note may inform several tasks, with each
completion and reward assessed under its own terms.

What “checked” should mean

Check a task's setup before listing it as open:
1. The starting material is accessible to the intended audience and pinned to
   exact note revisions. Private material is not exposed by being referenced.
2. The question and scope are intelligible. Its connection to a research
   direction is explicit, and an existing answer or duplicate has been checked.
3. The expected evidence, completion criteria and verification plan are stated.
   Check that the proposed verifier can assess the scoped deliverable, including
   tasks whose research direction or method is open-ended.
4. A poster is responsible for maintaining the brief and assessing responses.

This can be a pipeline-assisted process with escalation for uncertainty. It is
not blanket scientific endorsement, a promise that a solution exists, or a
requirement for experts to certify every underlying claim before discussion.
The UI must state what was actually checked and against which source versions.

Keep proposing lightweight: source note, task, completion criteria, verification
plan, background and openness. Unchecked proposals stay with the poster with usable
feedback. Anyone can continue contributing notes under the existing notebook
rules; bounty status does not control ordinary note participation.

Verification, including open-ended work

Openness describes the choices left to the researcher. Every bounty still needs
a way to assess completion. A broad search can yield a precisely checkable witness,
experiment, proof, or scoped counterexample. Agree the deliverable and checks when
the task opens, rather than judging an unbounded promise after someone contributes.

Prefer a runnable check for reproducible claims: pin code and inputs, record the
environment, and state the expected outcome or comparison. For mathematical claims,
specify the statement, assumptions and evidence to be independently checked. A
review-based verifier should use explicit criteria and record its reasoning and
unresolved issues. An inconclusive assessment must remain inconclusive.

The broadest example asks for a new research goal plus one substantiated initial
claim. Its plan calls for an independent re-run or proof/counterexample review.
Completion records that scoped evidence; it does not imply the whole proposed
research direction has been established. This keeps verification useful even when
the researcher chooses both the goal and the approach.

Each prototype brief now shows How it’s checked, with proposed checks and a
completion condition. New proposals require a verification plan and retain it in
their preview and saved draft. These are plans, not executable or already-run
verifiers. A production verifier should have a versioned specification, pinned
evidence and a recorded result. Its evidence informs the pipeline decision below;
it does not silently grant poster approval or award points.

Completion and approval

A contributor links a note revision and a short account of what it establishes.
A partial contribution remains visible even when it does not fulfill the task.
A claimed solution starts two distinct decisions:

  Poster approval: does this meet the task that was actually requested?
  Pipeline approval: does the evidence substantiate that scoped result under
  the applicable evaluation policy?

Neither decision implies the other. A submission cannot approve itself, remove
another person's task from discovery, or immediately grant points. The record
must show pending, approved, disputed and revision-needed states accurately.

Bind both approvals to the same task brief, verifier specification and exact
submitted revision/hash. If any changes materially, request a new assessment instead of carrying
approval across silently. The reward becomes eligible only when both approvals
hold. Record the award once, with an auditable receipt. If the poster contributed
the solution, an eligible substitute must handle the poster-side decision.

Disagreement should produce a concrete reason and a next action. An absent
poster must not leave a valid submission permanently in limbo: define a response
window and a steward/appeal route before launch. This fallback need not turn
routine awards into a central manual approval queue.

When a task closes, record what changed in its parent research question. A
reproduction, corrected assumption, counterexample or remaining obstacle should
be usable by the next researcher. Recommendations for a new scheme or challenge
must retain an owner and pass through the existing implementation/admission
process. Bounty completion does not itself deploy code or certify a scheme.

Points: the two options under consideration

A. A fixed number of points per credit.
   Easy to understand and audit, and stable while the work is in progress.
   However, equal awards can favor small tasks and repeated task splitting over
   difficult, consequential work. A common credit unit does not establish equal
   scientific value.

B. The pipeline estimates the points a contribution deserves.
   More sensitive to scope, evidence and value, including unexpected useful
   results. It requires calibration and explanations. If the only estimate
   arrives after submission, researchers do not know the reward they are working
   toward and the evaluation can feel arbitrary.

Recommendation for a pilot: test a few fixed reward tiers assigned during the
brief check, publish the amount before the task opens, and freeze that offer for
its stated criteria. Keep poster + pipeline approval as the completion gate in
either model. If later merit bonuses are desirable, distinguish them from the
advertised award so a promised reward cannot be reduced retrospectively.

Do not treat the scope rating as a points multiplier. An uncertain question can be
poorly chosen, and a simple reproduction can be consequential. Reward reasons
should refer to the task's contribution to the research record.

The draft uses clearly marked example point amounts to make reward labels and
sorting reviewable. They are neither calibrated awards nor a chosen reward policy.
New local proposals show Points not set; trying the prototype awards no points.
The previous “one credit for seven days” visibility model remains removed.
Credits may eventually fund or constrain bounties, but the exact funding, starter
grants, refund and posting-eligibility rules still need a decision. Ordinary
note participation should remain separate.

Keeping discovery useful over time

Keep a small, maintained brief separate from the growing evidence archive. Give
questions durable identities and retain earlier versions and closed outcomes.
Use bounded pages and stable ordering. Make room for varied tasks and research
directions; do not make reward size or accumulated popularity the
sole ranking rule. New work should have a route to attention.

Ask owners to refresh stale briefs. An outdated task can leave the default view
without erasing its evidence or treating a lack of response as a resolution.
Pending submissions preserve their review and reward commitments. A proposed
visibility cap or refresh interval is a separate policy from task completion.

Community flags, duplicate checks and visible award histories will be needed.
Two approvals are not proof of independence: conflicts, self-awards and repeated
reciprocal patterns need explicit handling. Test those rules in the beta before
making reputation control broad access to the product.

Implementation boundary

The local prototype demonstrates the proposed hierarchy and journeys using eight
fictional tasks: six open, one awaiting the example user's review, and one past.
It supports sorting, reading, local task proposals, linked contributions and
an example poster decision. It uses Crucible's existing notebook routing,
authentication ownership, asset loader, controls and styles.

There is no live task-checking engine, rewards ledger or points change. Pipeline
approvals and evidence in the fixture are explicitly examples. New local
proposals remain unchecked; local claims begin with both approvals pending.
Nothing is published by trying the prototype. Reload clears local changes.

For production, use versioned bounty terms and exact notebook references for
source and contribution evidence. Bounty records own checks, lifecycle, two
approval decisions, outcomes and reward receipts. This is the agreed architecture;
the fixture has not been migrated to production task or note records.
Keep transport, permissions and idempotent writes within the existing application
conventions. Independent state is necessary for these distinct decisions; a new
application shell, authentication layer or universal cache is not.

Next concrete milestone

Use one real task from the archive to exercise the whole journey: checked brief,
researcher discovers it, contribution note, both assessments, recorded outcome.
Measure whether people find suitable work, time to a useful response, reviewer
effort and what actually changes in the archive. Those observations should decide
the reward policy and the next iteration, rather than additional interface text.
